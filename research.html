Research Paper · Benchmarking & Evaluation
NetLLM-Eval: Benchmarking Large Language Models for Automated Fault Diagnosis and Root Cause Analysis in Enterprise Networks
Gowtham Saminathan · Independent Researcher · gowtham@gowthams.com
Abstract

Enterprise network operations generate vast quantities of unstructured data — incident logs, syslog streams, configuration diffs, and ticketing records — that exceed the capacity of human operators to process at speed and scale. This paper introduces NetLLM-Eval, a benchmark framework grounded in real-world enterprise network incidents, covering three core tasks: (1) fault symptom classification from raw syslog and SNMP data, (2) root cause identification from multi-source incident context, and (3) remediation step generation for common network failure scenarios. Drawing from practitioner experience across 500+ managed devices spanning Palo Alto, Cisco, Juniper, and Fortinet platforms, we evaluate GPT-4o, Llama-3, and Mistral under zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought prompting regimes. Our results demonstrate that domain-adapted prompting yields up to 78% accuracy on fault classification — outperforming zero-shot baselines by 23 percentage points — and identify systematic failure modes in multi-vendor configuration reasoning, providing a reproducible evaluation harness for the research community.

Position / Roadmap Paper · Analog & Optical Inference
Executing Trained Transformers on Low-Cost Physical Substrates: A Frugal Roadmap for Analog Electronic and Optical Inference
Gowtham · Independent Researcher · gowtham@gowthams.com
Abstract

The energy and memory cost of large language model (LLM) inference is dominated not by arithmetic but by moving weights between memory and processor. Analog in-memory and optical computing eliminate this movement by encoding trained weights as physical quantities — electrical conductances or optical transmittances — so that a matrix–vector multiplication (MVM) is performed by physics itself in effectively constant time. Progress, however, is concentrated in a small number of well-funded laboratories. This paper argues that the field's binding constraint is not access to exotic devices but the absence of an open, end-to-end toolchain for mapping quantized transformers onto small, noisy, reusable physical tiles. We contribute (i) a reference hybrid analog–digital architecture built around weight-stationary tiles with time-multiplexed "tile-and-reuse" weight virtualization; (ii) a staged implementation methodology with a total bill of materials under US$500, spanning open-source noise-aware simulation, an 8×8 digital-potentiometer crossbar, and an incoherent optical multiplier built from printed transparency masks; (iii) a pre-registered evaluation protocol; and (iv) a cost and scaling analysis identifying data converters and reprogramming overhead — not the analog cores — as the true bottlenecks. We conclude that the most scalable contribution available to independent researchers is the compiler and calibration stack, validated on deliberately inexpensive hardware.

Contributions

  • Reference architecture for hybrid analog–digital transformer inference organized around weight-stationary tiles, differential nonnegative weight encoding, hardware-in-the-loop calibration, and tile-and-reuse weight virtualization.
  • Staged methodology with four phases — noise-aware simulation, an electronic crossbar tile, an incoherent optical tile, and a hybrid transformer block — each with explicit deliverables and cost under $500 total.
  • Pre-registered evaluation protocol defining metrics (task fidelity, effective precision, energy, throughput, stability), baselines, and a sim-to-real gap measurement.
  • Cost and scaling analysis quantifying why data converters and reprogramming latency, not the analog medium, dominate total cost.

Evaluation Metrics (Pre-Registered)

  • M1 — Task fidelity: Classification accuracy (MNIST) and perplexity delta vs. FP32 baseline, with 95% confidence intervals over ≥ 10 runs.
  • M2 — Effective precision: ENOB of the physical MVM, derived from measured SNDR per output channel.
  • M3 — Energy: Energy per MAC measured at the wall, decomposed into analog core, DACs/ADCs, and orchestrator.
  • M4 — Throughput: MVMs per second and end-to-end seconds per generated token, including reprogramming time.
  • M5 — Stability: Drift of M1 and M2 over 24 hours and ±10 °C ambient sweep, with and without recalibration.

Key Insight

Two costs dominate, and neither is the analog medium. First, conversion: every physical MVM is bracketed by DACs and ADCs, and in small systems the converters consume the large majority of total energy. Second, reprogramming under virtualization: a 7-billion-parameter model contains ≈ 10⁸ tiles of size 8×8. Frugal tile-and-reuse therefore cannot target competitive throughput; it targets architectural fidelity — proof that the compiler, calibration, and noise-adaptation stack is correct — on hardware anyone can afford.
